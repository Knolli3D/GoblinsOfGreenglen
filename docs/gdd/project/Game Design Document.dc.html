Purpose and source hierarchy

This GDD is the product-design baseline: what the game should feel like, why its systems exist, how future content fits together. When documents disagree, priority is: (1) the running game/code define implemented behavior, (2) this GDD defines design intent, (3) AGENTS.md/CLAUDE.md define technical constraints, (4) README.md is the public feature summary, (5) plan.md holds completed campaign-map prompts, (6) Plan_todo.txt is exploration, not approved behavior.

1. High Concept

Elevator pitch

Goblins of Greenglen is an approachable fantasy platformer: a nimble knight bounds across floating paths, stomps mischievous goblins, gathers coins, and races toward the next red flag. Short, readable levels feed replayable runs, local records, campaign mastery, quests, and a cosmetic collection.

Player promise

  • Movement easy to understand, satisfying to improve
  • Combat that turns accurate platforming into a playful attack
  • Compact levels, visible goals, little downtime
  • Clear reasons to replay: score, time, map completion, quests, cosmetics
  • Warm, whimsical medieval-fantasy identity — never grim or punishing

Product identity

Sits between a light arcade platformer and a compact progression game. Platforming must stay enjoyable without the meta systems; quests, cases, skins, campaign milestones, and records strengthen replayability but never conceal weak core play.

2. Design Pillars

2.1 Readable momentum

Most of a level is spent moving, jumping, lining up stomps, or choosing a route. Hazards stay legible so failure reads as a decision or execution error: controls respond immediately, double jump gives recovery and expressive routing, enemies patrol predictably, the goal flag is visually distinct, and non-interactive interruptions stay brief.

2.2 Stomping is platforming

Combat is not a separate system — position, descent, timing, and horizontal overlap ARE the attack. A successful stomp feels precise but generous: immediate bounce, score feedback, sound, and a “POW!” flourish.

2.3 One more clean run

Runs are short enough to restart willingly, layered with independent goals: complete the route, improve Final Score, lower Run Time, finish quests, clear without damage, perfect campaign regions.

2.4 Storybook Greenglen

Bright, readable platforming inside ornate, painterly fantasy framing. Wood, dark metal, gold trim, leaves, vines, shields, castles, keys, chests, mushrooms, and saturated rarity colors form the Greenglen visual language.

2.5 Progress is earned, not purchased

Gameplay and quest completion drive the cosmetic economy. Cases provide anticipation; duplicate shards ensure an unlucky reward still has value. No real-money economy in the current design.

2.6 Expand without invalidating the foundation

Stable region/level identities, controller boundaries, and separate save domains let the game grow. New regions and modes reuse the strong core instead of silently changing what “Start Game” means.

3. Audience, Positioning, and Scope

Target audience

  • Players who enjoy accessible 2D platformers and score chasing
  • Players drawn to colorful medieval fantasy and collectible cosmetics
  • Players who prefer short sessions and clear progress
  • Younger/casual players needing forgiving recovery, alongside mastery goals for experienced players

Desired difficulty character

Easy to enter, increasingly demanding, fair to master. Early levels teach movement and stomp rules at low density. Later levels raise horizontal scale, enemy density, routing pressure, and variability — without changing the player's fundamental physics.

Current scope

Single-player, offline-first, desktop keyboard, six playable levels in one released campaign region, local saves/records, no networking/accounts/cloud/real-money/online leaderboard.

Non-goals for the current foundation

Precision-platformer difficulty as default; complex melee combos/weapons/inventory stats; multiplayer/PvP; a dialogue-heavy narrative campaign; mandatory grinding to access the core six levels; pay-to-win.

4. Player Fantasy, World, and Tone

The player is a heroic fantasy character crossing the lively realm of Greenglen, outmaneuvering goblins and claiming a path through dangerous locations. The fantasy is carried through movement, visual identity, and playful confrontation rather than exposition.

Tone

Adventurous, mischievous, triumphant, colorful, lightly comedic — never excessively violent or bleak. Goblins read as troublesome, animated obstacles, not horror creatures. Defeat feedback is punchy and amusing; success feels celebratory without slowing the next attempt.

Narrative canon

Protagonist: a farmer's kid from Greenglen Vale who picked up a sword because nobody else would — no formal training, no chosen-one framing, just the nearest person willing to go. Inciting incident: goblins raided the Harvest Festival and made off with the mayor's secret pie recipe; emboldened (and well-fed), scattered raiders swelled into a full occupation of the Vale as more bands arrived for a taste. The flags: not goblin markers at all — they're waymarkers of the long-vanished Silvergreen Kingdom, predating the goblins entirely; restoring them re-opens the old royal road and unlocks the route between regions on the map. The region journey: escalating threat, played for atmosphere rather than horror — the goblins aren't invading so much as running, region by region, from something older and stranger than themselves; each biome hides a bigger reason they kept moving, culminating in whatever they were fleeing in the first place at the far edge of the ice. Tone stays light and comedic through Region 2; Regions 3–5 may lean into quieter, eerier atmosphere (old carvings, deep cold, things half-remembered) but never into real menace or grimness — goblins stay silly, danger stays implied, not shown.

World and characters

  • Grubnash, the Pie King — self-appointed goblin leader who kicked off the invasion out of stolen-pie bravado; mostly bluster, no real menace, in keeping with the tone.
  • The Silvergreen Princesses — Greenglen Vale's reigning royal line; the case/key reward shop is literally their treasury, opened to fund the knight's campaign. Collectible princess skins are an honor granted for service, not a rescue plot.
  • Sir Alderic — a retired knight and mentor who explains regions and pie lore from the map screen; sideline voice only, never adventures directly.
  • The Old Silvergreen Road — the ancient royal road the flags waymark; recurs across all five (and future) regions as the visible thread physically connecting them on the map.

Ending: intentionally open past Region 5 — the old danger driving the escalating-threat journey is not resolved at the ice biome, and further regions beyond Region 5 remain TBD to carry it forward. Still open: naming that further danger, and any landmarks/allies/antagonists beyond the above. Future narrative work should favor environmental detail, map flavor, short introductions, and concise milestones over dialogue-heavy delivery.

5. Experience Structure

5.1 Moment-to-moment loop

Read nearby platforms/enemies/coins → move and jump toward the route → use the second jump to extend/correct/redirect → choose to avoid or stomp a goblin → collect coins toward the flag → recover from a mistake or restart.

5.2 Run loop

Start with 3 hearts, zero score, zero coins, fresh timer → complete levels via goal flags → carry score/coins/time across transitions → finish the route or lose all health → review Final Score and Run Time → run again, return to menu, or chase another goal.

5.3 Campaign loop

Open the Map → review region status/routes/records/requirements → launch an unlocked location → complete it and unlock the next → clear the region once all main locations and required core trials are done → return for optional exploration/mastery.

5.4 Meta-progression loop (cosmetic only — never affects movement, health, damage, score, or odds)

Play levels & runs→ Advance daily/weekly quests→ Claim keys / fragments→ Open regular / premium cases→ Unlock & equip skins

Cases also convert duplicate rewards into shards → more keys, feeding back into further case openings; equipped skins feed back into more runs.

6. Game Modes and Entry Points

6.1 Classic Run Implemented

Start Game begins the linear six-level run at Location 1, played in order. Location 6 completion yields Run Complete; zero hearts yields Run Over. Completed runs may set Best Score, Best Time, both, or neither — failed runs never update either record. R, pause Try Again, and result Run Again all start a clean run at Location 1. This remains the default “whole game” challenge.

6.2 Campaign Map Implemented foundation

Map opens the campaign submenu. Players inspect all planned regions; only released/unlocked locations can launch gameplay. Starting an unlocked Region 1 location continues through subsequent required locations to the region endpoint — the mode doesn't return to the map after every level, nor auto-cross into a successor region. Locked/unreleased locations stay visible for orientation. The map remembers the last valid selection.

6.3 Endless Challenge Candidate

Plan_todo.txt describes an arcade survival concept: cycles the six templates with rising difficulty, persistent run health, larger arcade scoring, death-only finalization, and a future leaderboard-ready result object. Treat as an additional mode candidate, not a Classic Run/Campaign replacement, unless a later decision says otherwise. Prototype should answer: does cycling stay fun beyond two loops? Is persistent 3-heart health too punishing without recovery? Does scoring stay comparable with random spawns? Do endless-compatible quests coexist with campaign quests? Is an online leaderboard worth its cost? No networking or leaderboard is currently approved.

7.3 Movement design rules

Horizontal movement is direct and predictable; the first jump provides the main arc, the second provides correction and expression. Level geometry is authored against the existing physics unless a deliberate global rebalance is approved. Required routes never depend on random spawns. A player should be able to read why a jump failed from the visible arc and landing point. Camera behavior preserves forward visibility and never reveals space beyond level bounds.

7.4 Input roadmap — TBD

Controller support, full key rebinding, input prompt switching, configurable hold/toggle behavior, touch controls or console certification.

7.5 Planned ability — Wall Jump Planned

Region 2's (Stonepeak Reach) signature traversal ability, layered on top of — never replacing — the existing double jump. Pressing toward a designated climbable-wall surface while airborne and falling catches a slow wall-slide; jumping while sliding kicks the knight off and away at a set arc. The kick is its own move, tracked separately from the double jump, and always refreshes it exactly like a ground touch — a wall kick followed by one double jump is guaranteed, never consumed by the kick itself. A brief lockout prevents immediately re-grabbing the same wall right after a kick, so a single wall can't be cheesed into infinite height; landing on solid ground clears the lockout immediately, and it never restricts jumping to a different wall — chained ping-pong wall-jumping between two facing walls is the intended core read for Region 2's shafts, not a spam case to dampen. Climbable surfaces are an explicit tagged group authored per level, not a property of all vertical geometry, keeping Wall Jump a Region 2+ authored feature rather than a change to how existing levels read; a dedicated physics layer is only worth adding later if the tagged-group approach genuinely can't scale. This is a deliberate rebalance under §7.3 and needs its own tuning/playtest pass before any Region 2 blockout locks in geometry — that pass must include input forgiveness (a short jump-buffer window and a brief wall coyote-time grace after leaving a climbable surface), since wall jumps read as unresponsive very quickly once the input window is too literal. Starting values to prototype from: wall-slide capped near 120px/s downward, kick velocity around −480px/s vertical and 260px/s horizontal away from the wall, roughly 250ms re-grab lockout. Camera behavior must extend §7.3's “never reveal space beyond level bounds” rule to vertical tracking — per §18, a vertical-camera/level-bounds test is a prerequisite for this prototype, not a follow-on task, since today's camera and fall handling are horizontal-only assumptions that won't survive a level taller than one screen unmodified.

8. Combat, Damage, and Recovery

8.1 Stomp combat

Valid only when descending and crossing the top of a living goblin with sufficient overlap.

PropertyValue
Goblin patrol speed60 px/s
Stomp top tolerance2 px
Min. horizontal stomp overlap4 px

On a valid stomp: the goblin is defeated exactly once, combat score +1, the player bounces at double-jump velocity, both jumps refresh, stomp audio plays with pitch variation, quest progress advances, and a “POW!” effect appears. Upward/side contact or insufficient overlap counts as a hit instead. The swept contact test accounts for both actors' movement to reduce tunneling and frame-rate-dependent outcomes.

8.2 Health and damage

A fresh level load begins with 3 hearts. Enemy damage removes one heart and 1 combat-score point; falling does the same and respawns the player if health remains. A non-fatal hit/fall grants 1 second of invulnerability. New level loads have no spawn protection by design — spawns are placed away from enemies. Reaching 0 hearts ends the run exactly once. Planned Non-enemy environmental hazards (Region 3 rolling carts/kegs, §12.5) use this identical hit rule on contact — never stompable, never a second damage system.

8.3 Fairness rules

A dead or already-rewarded enemy cannot damage the player or award a second stomp. Spawn points never overlap enemy patrol areas; enemy patrol stays on its supporting platform. Invulnerability should be visually readable if future feedback is added (exact treatment TBD). A fatal event freezes the run before late callbacks can change the outcome.

9. Score, Coins, Time, and Records

ResourceRulePurpose
Hearts3 at level load; −1 per hit/fallImmediate survival margin
Combat Score+1 per stomp; −1 per hit/fallRewards combat cleanliness
Coins+1 per pickup, carried across the runRoute incentive, major score input
Run TimeActive gameplay milliseconds onlyIndependent speed measure

Final Score = max(0, Combat Score) + (Coins × 10)

Coins are intentionally worth more than stomps in the final result. Combat Score and Coins stay separately visible in play and separately recorded for level bests. The timer counts only active gameplay — not menus, pause, or the 1-second “Level Cleared!” delay; normal level changes retain elapsed time; both completion and failure freeze the result time.

Records

Best Score replaced only by a strictly higher Final Score; Best Time replaced only by a lower positive time; the two are independent and only completed runs are submitted. A failed run never changes either record. Legacy score records without a timed completion show “No best time yet.” Each stable location separately stores its best local score/coins (higher score first, then more coins on a tie).

Balance questions — TBD

Should combat score stay ±1 as enemy types grow? Does Final Score need a completion/no-damage/exploration/time component? Do coins stay score-only or gain a spendable purpose? Should campaign records add a time component? Resolve through playtest data, not hidden complexity.

10. Run Outcomes and Lifecycle

StateTriggerRecord policy
ActiveFresh run or level in progressNo submission
Run CompleteFinal required level reachedSubmit score and time
Run OverHealth reaches zeroNever submit
AbandonedRestart, menu exit, or replacementNever submit

Both final outcomes share one result hierarchy over the frozen final frame: outcome title, Final Score, Run Time, saved Best Score/Time status, completion-only “New Highscore!”/“New Best Time!”, and Run Again/Main Menu actions. Run Again gets initial keyboard focus; Pause cannot open over a result.

Exactly-once rule: result finalization, record submission, completion quests, and transitions must be idempotent — repeated fatal contacts, goal signals, stale callbacks, or input must never produce duplicate rewards or level loads.

Greenglen Vale and Stonepeak Reach are adopted working names (see Section 25). Regions 3–5 titles are early placeholders only — not canon until a content/naming pass confirms them.

11.2 Connection language

Required (solid): advances the main route and region-clear requirement. Optional (dotted): bonus exploration, never required for “cleared.” Locked/undiscovered (dimmed): visual state only, same semantic line.

11.3 Region milestone model

Cleared: all main locations + all core trials. Explored: Cleared + all bonus locations. Mastered: all main + bonus + mastery trials. Region 1 has no bonus locations or mastery trials, so Explored/Mastered follow Cleared automatically.

11.4 Region 1 gate

Requires all six main locations complete, plus Flawless Finale: clear Greenglen Crest (Location 6) without damage on that attempt. Does not require an entirely damage-free six-level run. Progress is capped and idempotent.

11.5 Region availability states

Available: released and unlocked, play unlocked locations. Locked: predecessor requirements remain, inspect dimmed topology. Coming Soon: predecessor cleared but region unreleased, preview only. An unreleased region stays non-playable even once its gate is earned.

11.6 Future region rules

Give every region a distinct mechanical identity, not just new art. Teach one or two new ideas early, combine them later, reserve mastery demands for optional paths/trials. Don't define fictional core trials before the real mechanics exist. Don't release a region until every required location has a valid scene and reachable route. Keep stable IDs once save data references them. Optional branches should offer a real variation: route challenge, collectible focus, unusual enemy mix, or mastery test.

12.2 Level construction rules

Scenes stay editable in Godot's 2D editor. Required jumps are tested against current movement constants. PlayerSpawn stays safely separated from enemies and falls. The goal flag stays reachable and visually clear. Coins support route readability, optional risk, or reward skilled arcs. Enemies create timing/landing decisions rather than unavoidable damage. Scrolling stages keep the player within camera bounds. The final approach to a goal never places an unavoidable enemy under the goal trigger.

12.3 Randomized spawns (Greenglen Crest / Location 6)

Only spawn_platforms-group platforms participate; start/goal platforms stay out of the pool. Patrol ranges are chosen within a safe range, constrained to platform bounds. Enemies use one per platform before cycling if more are requested. Multiple coins may share a platform; vertical offsets match the authored convention. A retry creates a new arrangement — no seed persists. Randomization may change tactical routing but never makes the level impossible.

12.4 Vertical level checkpoints Planned

Levels authored taller than a single screen (Region 2 climbs, some Region 3 shafts) may place one or more checkpoint markers along the critical path. Touching one updates the active respawn point for the current attempt only — nothing persists across a retry or a new run. A later fall or hit still costs a heart per §8.2, but respawn moves to the most recent checkpoint instead of level start, keeping a tall climb's fall risk from stacking on Region 1's single-spawn punishment. This is the target policy, not yet the implemented one: today a fall already respawns the player, but an ordinary enemy/hazard hit only cancels vertical velocity rather than triggering a respawn — the two pathways must converge on this single checkpoint-aware respawn policy before Region 2 ships, not just for falls. Checkpoints never sit above a hazard or mid-air over empty space, and the last checkpoint before a goal still respects the “no unavoidable enemy under the goal trigger” rule above. A no-damage trial (e.g. Flawless Ascent) is still judged from true level start regardless of checkpoints reached during a normal clear — checkpoint activation banks position only and must never clear a level's no-damage tracking (e.g. a took_damage_this_level flag), so touching one can never accidentally launder trial progress. Visual/audio confirmation on activation is open (§23).

12.5 Rolling hazards (Region 3) Planned

Ore carts and ale kegs move along authored rails or slopes at a fixed, predictable speed — never spawned out of view and never accelerated at random. A cart/keg schedule may run as a continuous timed loop (good for an ambient background rail) or fire once per player trigger (better for a curated teaching beat) — both stay fully deterministic as long as their initial conditions are fixed, and either is valid per level. Each must be visible or audible at least one full beat before it becomes relevant to the player's route — not merely before it exists somewhere off-screen — per pillar 2.1's readability rule, and successive hazards on a shared route stay spaced farther apart than the 1-second invulnerability window (§8.2), so a level can never be cleared by tanking straight through one hazard into the next. Contact applies the identical “hit” rule as an enemy (§8.2): −1 heart, −1 combat score, 1 second of invulnerability — but hazards get their own contact-detection source rather than reusing the swept check the player runs against the enemies group, and stay out of that group entirely, so a hazard can never be stomped, never awards combat score, and never counts as a kill, even though its contact event routes into the same player-damage entry point goblins use. Hazards are never stompable or otherwise defeatable — the only valid response is timing and positioning. A required route never depends on a hazard's random behavior, because hazards don't have any: identical layout every attempt. A level introducing carts or kegs for the first time keeps nearby goblin density low — a new hazard read and dense combat are taught separately, then combined only once each is independently familiar.

13. Quests and Reward Economy

13.1 Quest structure

3 daily slots from a pool of 7; 2 weekly slots from a pool of 4. Daily quests reset by calendar day; claiming all three active dailies immediately rolls a fresh set. Progress counts gameplay actions, not Final Score points. Daily goals cover stomps, coin collection, a no-damage goal, full-run completion, double jumps, and level clears; weekly goals cover completed runs, stomps, coins, and no-damage runs.

13.2 Keys and fragments

Keys come only from claimed quests, never purchased with coins. The first 6 daily claims in a day grant one full key each; further claims grant a fragment. 3 fragments auto-convert to 1 key. A claimed weekly quest grants 3 keys.

13.3 Cases

CaseCostRareEpicLegendary
Regular1 key60%30%10%
Premium3 keys55%30%15%

Opening uses a decelerating reel and rarity-scaled reveal feedback. Premium improves Legendary odds but doesn't guarantee beating three Regular openings every time.

13.4 Duplicate protection

An owned skin awards 1 duplicate shard; 10 shards auto-convert to 1 key. Duplicate value is intentionally weaker than quest-fragment value, so cases stay a collection sink, not a self-sustaining farm.

13.5 Economy design rules

The core game stays playable with zero case interaction. Cosmetics never modify gameplay outcomes. Costs/odds are always visible before spending keys. Random rewards never consume real money under the current design. A player always retains the Default Knight and starter skin. Save normalization never removes legitimately owned cosmetics. New skins should weigh duplicate dilution and completion pacing before launch.

Skin design rules

Every skin needs transparent source art. Collision and movement stay unchanged. Silhouette stays readable at in-game height. Rarity reads through craftsmanship, color treatment, and reveal presentation — never gameplay power. New skins need a unique stable ID, display name, tier, texture, collection behavior, preview, and save-compatibility check. Avoid low-value hue swaps that vanish during normal play.

Skin canon — knights and princesses of old

In-fiction, every skin (Default Knight excepted) is a preserved likeness of a real figure from the Silvergreen Kingdom's past — a knight or princess who walked Greenglen generations before the current goblin trouble. Equipping one means the player's knight dons that figure's remembered armor and bearing, not their skill: appearance and spirit only transfer, never stats, which is why cosmetics stay purely visual by design. The Default Knight carries no such memory — it's simply the knight as they are right now, present-day and untitled.

15. UI and Information Architecture

15.1 Main navigation

Start Game · Map · Quests · Cases · Skins · Quit Game. Exact ordering may evolve, but Start Game remains the primary action and Map the campaign entry point.

15.2 Gameplay HUD

Communicates current level context, hearts, combat score, coins, active Run Time, short transient messages, and stomp feedback. Stays readable over bright and dark backgrounds through pale text, dark outlines, spacing, and consistent anchoring.

15.3 Menu-state priority

Only one major context is active at a time, in priority order: result, pause, campaign/meta submenu, main menu, gameplay HUD. Opening one hides incompatible contexts, clears stale gameplay when appropriate, and restores sensible keyboard focus.

15.4 Map UX

One reused map shell; region selector as primary navigation; status banner in the header; visible solid required / dotted optional routes; selectable locked/unreleased nodes for inspection; a disabled, silent Play action for anything unplayable; a details panel for the selected location; remembered last selection; clean Back navigation.

15.5 Accessibility and usability roadmap — TBD

Controller navigation/rebinding, separate music/SFX volume, reduced screen shake/flash, high-contrast and color-independent rarity indicators, scalable UI/text beyond viewport stretch, visible invulnerability feedback, subtitles/text equivalents for audio cues, pause-on-focus-loss, localization-safe layouts, assisted-mode options if playtests show repeated early abandonment. Rarity, map status, and health must never rely on color alone.

16. Art Direction

Visual thesis: a bright platforming storybook framed by a richly illustrated medieval-fantasy interface. Gameplay prioritizes silhouette and spatial clarity; menus carry the ornate fantasy spectacle.

Existing visual language

Lush green valleys, forests, mountains, rivers, castles, ruins, caves; painterly backdrops with atmospheric depth; crisp floating stone-and-grass platforms; saturated gold coins and a simple red flag; expressive green goblins and detailed knight/princess art; dark wood panels framed by vines, roots, stone, dark metal; gold keys, chests, crystals, mushrooms, shields, leaf crests, warm lantern light; ornate 6:1 buttons with normal/hover/pressed/disabled states; Cinzel Bold titles, Cinzel SemiBold buttons/body; pale cream text with dark brown outlines.

Palette roles

Composition rules

Reserve a calm area behind UI text/controls. Use ornate framing at edges and menu anchors without covering the core interaction area. Preserve strong foreground/midground/background separation. Keep collectibles/hazards distinguishable from the backdrop at gameplay scale. Use gold for reward, completion, legendary rarity, and primary emphasis. Keep failure warm and readable without turning the tone grim. New menu backgrounds belong to the same illustrated world and repeat the leaf-crest identity.

Character/object scale

Knight art targets 52px in-game height; goblin art targets 40px; platform art scales to its collision rectangle. Source images may be high-resolution but must stay clean and readable reduced to gameplay scale.

Environment roadmap

Future regions should each define: biome and time/weather identity, foreground framing motifs, platform-material family, background depth layers, landmark/destination, enemy/collectible contrast plan, map-node/banner accent, and transition relationship to neighboring regions. A future true parallax setup needs foreground art with transparent sky areas — two opaque full-frame backgrounds must not be stacked and called depth.

17. Audio Direction

Playful generated chiptune: energetic, slightly goofy, intentionally lighter than the richly painted menu art. Reinforces arcade pace and keeps defeat from feeling harsh.

Current event set

Looping music; jump; double jump; coin; stomp; hit; death; level clear; win; UI click. Coin/stomp/jump use slight pitch jitter to reduce repetition. Eight round-robin SFX voices allow overlap.

Mixing rules

Music and SFX use separate buses; Music bus sits at −6dB; pause ducks music a further 14dB via the audio controller. Resume/restart/main-menu return always restore the normal music level. Run completion/failure stops gameplay music before the result presentation.

Future audio rules

New region music keeps melodic chiptune continuity while reflecting biome identity. Essential events need unique, short, non-fatiguing cues. Rarity-reveal audio scales in excitement without masking the reward name. No single effect should dominate after bus routing and simultaneous playback. Volume sliders and mute controls are a priority usability addition.

18. Technical Design Constraints

Godot 4.6 standard build; pure GDScript, no C#; 960×540 internal viewport; 1280×720 default window; canvas_items stretch mode; no external plugins or runtime dependencies.

Ownership boundaries

Game.gd owns run state, active stable IDs, level lifecycle, transitions, score, coins, health, timing, outcomes. Explicit scene-tree controllers own audio, HUD, menus, quests, cases, skins, campaign map, campaign progress, and highscores. UI sends intent signals; it doesn't own competing gameplay state. CampaignCatalog.gd is the source of truth for region/location/trial/connection/scene-path/map metadata. Progression.gd is the sole deliberate autoload, since meta-progression must exist across scenes and menus. Player-to-game combat/movement events stay signal-based. Planned rolling hazards (§12.5) get their own contact-detection source, distinct from the swept check the player already runs against the enemies group, and stay outside that group entirely while still calling into the same player-damage entry point — one damage policy, never two competing systems.

Design-facing invariants

Stable IDs are never renamed post-release without migration. Unreleased content never becomes playable just because it's visible. Level-changing, menu, restart, and run-ending paths invalidate delayed transitions. Rewards and saves are exactly-once under duplicate signals. New menus reuse the shared theme and ownership model. Core gameplay stays offline-capable.

Vertical-level prerequisites (Region 2+) Planned

Today's camera and fall handling are horizontal-only assumptions: a hardcoded 540px camera bottom, a fixed y > 700 fall/kill threshold, and background art effectively built for a single 540px-tall band. None of that survives a level taller than one screen unmodified. Before any Region 2 blockout locks in geometry, this needs authored per-level camera bounds and kill bounds replacing those hardcoded constants, plus a vertical-safe background treatment (tiling, extension art, or a redesigned parallax rig). This is a prerequisite, not a parallel task — the Wall Jump mechanic can prototype and feel correct in an isolated test scene while the level-presentation layer underneath it is still broken (§7.5, §21, §22).

20. Quality Bar and Validation

443 deterministic headless checks currently cover save-system, campaign-progress, and smoke/behavior suites, run in isolated child processes with a canary protecting real save files. Test counts should only be updated from actual final test output.

Feature definition of done

Intended player outcome documented; happy path, failure path, restart, menu exit, and duplicate-input behavior defined; keyboard focus and 960×540 layout verified; save compatibility considered where state persists; automated regression coverage exists; parser/import and normal headless startup pass; visual/audio feedback matches the existing style; public and technical docs stay in sync.

Level definition of done

Loads from the catalog and is reachable through the intended flow; all required jumps are consistently possible; spawn/goal/collision/camera/fall/retry behavior is correct; enemy patrols stay on valid surfaces; collectibles are reachable and purposeful; completable with and without optional risks; no-damage completion is possible where a trial needs it; art has no seams, opaque boxes, or unreadable overlaps; playtested for first-clear and repeat-clear experience.

Playtest questions

Where do first-timers misread stomp direction? Do players discover and use the double jump intentionally? Which coins read as route guidance vs. arbitrary placement? Where does damage feel unavoidable? Do players understand Combat Score vs. Final Score? Can they explain why failed runs skip records? Can they tell Locked from Coming Soon on the map? Do quests motivate another run or distract from platforming? Are case costs/odds understood before opening? Does randomized Location 6 feel fresh or unfair? Telemetry isn't implemented — playtest notes should avoid collecting unnecessary personal data.
